# Language Transfer Marathi — Lesson 1, SKETCH (v0)

**Written by:** Claude, who does NOT speak Marathi. This is a directional sketch based on partial knowledge of Marathi structure. **It needs native-speaker QA before it can be trusted as anything more than a starting point.**

**Audience:** English speaker married into a Marathi-speaking family. Wants to talk with in-laws at the dinner table.
**Medium:** Audio only.
**Target length:** ~7 minutes.
**Frame:** *मला ___ हवं* (malā ___ havam) — "I want ___"
**Structural features deliberately demonstrated:** SOV word order, dative-experiencer construction for *want*, pronoun set in dative, negation by particle replacement.

| Element | What I'm saying | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| मला (malā) = to me / I-want-subject | yes | HIGH |
| पाणी (pāṇī) = water, neuter gender | yes | HIGH |
| दूध (dūdh) = milk, neuter | yes | HIGH |
| जेवण (jevaṇ) = food/meal, neuter | yes | MED — *anna* is more formal, *jevaṇ* is more everyday |
| हवं (havam) = wanted, neuter form | yes | MED — neuter agrees with neuter nouns, but is colloquial form correct? Standard would be हवे (have) |
| नको (nako) = not-wanted | yes | MED — verify it replaces havam directly |
| आहे (āhe) = is, copula at end | often dropped colloquially | MED |
| तुला (tulā) = to you (informal) | yes | HIGH |
| त्याला (tyālā) = to him | yes | HIGH |
| तिला (tilā) = to her | yes | HIGH |

**The single biggest thing for the QA-rater (your spouse / in-law) to verify:** Is *मला पाणी हवं* (*mala paani havam*) the actual sentence a native speaker uses when asking for water at the dinner table — or would they say *मला पाणी हवं आहे* with the *āhe* at the end, or *मला पाणी पाहिजे* (mala paani pahije) with a different verb entirely? The colloquial-vs-formal question is the make-or-break for whether this lesson reads as real Marathi.

## SCRIPT

---

### Track 1 — Open (00:00)

**Teacher:** Marathi and English aren't related languages in any close way, but they share enough through history that you'll have things to grab onto. There's one big difference between them that you'll feel from the first sentence: the order of the words. English puts the verb in the middle — *I want water.* Marathi puts the verb at the end — closer to *I water want.* Once you have that one move, a lot of the rest of the language falls into place. So let's start there.

---

### Track 2 — The first frame (00:30)

**Teacher:** The word for *I* in Marathi, when you're talking about wanting something, is **मला**. *Malā.* Two short sounds. *Ma-lā.* Say it.

**Student:** Malā.

**Teacher:** Good. *Malā.* And here's something interesting about that word — it doesn't quite mean *I.* It means closer to *to me.* Marathi treats wanting as something that happens *to* you, not something you *do*. You don't want water in Marathi. To you, water is wanted. We'll feel that as we build the sentence.

**Teacher:** The word for *water* in Marathi is **पाणी**. *Pāṇī.* Two short sounds. *Pā-ṇī.*

**Student:** Pāṇī.

**Teacher:** Good. And the word for *wanted* — like *needed*, *desired* — is **हवं**. *Havam.* Short, one breath.

**Student:** Havam.

**Teacher:** Good. So now put them together. To say *I want water*, you say *to-me, water, wanted.* In that order. *Malā pāṇī havam.* Try it.

*[pause]*

**Student:** Malā pāṇī havam.

**Teacher:** Yes. *Malā pāṇī havam.* Three words. *Malā* — to me. *Pāṇī* — water. *Havam* — wanted. The verb is at the end. The thing you want is in the middle. And the *I* doesn't really exist as a separate idea — it's already inside *malā*, which means *to me*.

---

### Track 3 — Swap the noun (02:30)

**Teacher:** The word for *milk* in Marathi is **दूध**. *Dūdh.* One sound — *dūdh.*

**Student:** Dūdh.

**Teacher:** Good. So how would you say *I want milk*?

*[pause]*

**Student:** Malā dūdh havam.

**Teacher:** Yes. *Malā dūdh havam.* Same frame. New thing in the middle. The word for *food*, or *a meal*, is **जेवण**. *Jevaṇ.* Two short sounds.

**Student:** Jevaṇ.

**Teacher:** Good. *I want food*?

*[pause]*

**Student:** Malā jevaṇ havam.

**Teacher:** Good. *Malā jevaṇ havam.*

**Teacher:** Now back and forth. *I want water*?

*[pause]*

**Student:** Malā pāṇī havam.

**Teacher:** *I want milk*?

*[pause]*

**Student:** Malā dūdh havam.

**Teacher:** *I want food*?

*[pause]*

**Student:** Malā jevaṇ havam.

**Teacher:** Good. Same frame. Different word in the middle. You can keep swapping any noun in there.

---

### Track 4 — Pronoun swap (04:00)

**Teacher:** Now let's meet somebody else. So far it's been *to me*. What about *to you*?

**Teacher:** The word for *to you*, talking to one person you're close with — like family — is **तुला**. *Tulā.* Two sounds, similar shape to *malā*. *Tu-lā.*

**Student:** Tulā.

**Teacher:** Good. Notice the *-lā* at the end is the same as on *malā*. That *-lā* is doing the *to* work. So if you can hear *-lā* on the end of a pronoun, you know it's a *to* form. You'll see it again.

**Teacher:** So how would you say *you want water*?

*[pause]*

**Student:** Tulā pāṇī havam.

**Teacher:** Yes. *Tulā pāṇī havam.* Same frame. Just changed the front.

**Teacher:** The word for *to him* is **त्याला**. *Tyālā.* Three sounds. *Tyā-lā.* And the word for *to her* is **तिला**. *Tilā.* Two sounds. *Ti-lā.*

**Student:** Tyālā. Tilā.

**Teacher:** Good. So — *he wants water*?

*[pause]*

**Student:** Tyālā pāṇī havam.

**Teacher:** *She wants milk*?

*[pause]*

**Student:** Tilā dūdh havam.

**Teacher:** *You want food*?

*[pause]*

**Student:** Tulā jevaṇ havam.

**Teacher:** Good. And notice — *havam* didn't change. *Pāṇī*, *dūdh*, *jevaṇ* didn't change. The only thing that moved was the pronoun at the front. The frame holds.

---

### Track 5 — Negation (05:30)

**Teacher:** Now. *I don't want.* In English we squeeze a *don't* into the middle of the sentence. Marathi does something simpler. It just swaps out the *wanted* word for a *not-wanted* word. The word for *not wanted* is **नको**. *Nako.* Two short sounds.

**Student:** Nako.

**Teacher:** Good. *Nako.* It replaces *havam* in the same spot at the end of the sentence. So *I don't want water* is *Malā pāṇī nako.* Try it.

**Student:** Malā pāṇī nako.

**Teacher:** Good. *Malā pāṇī nako.* The frame is exactly the same as before. The only thing that changed is the last word. *Havam* — wanted. *Nako* — not wanted.

**Teacher:** *I don't want milk*?

*[pause]*

**Student:** Malā dūdh nako.

**Teacher:** *I don't want food*?

*[pause]*

**Student:** Malā jevaṇ nako.

**Teacher:** Good. Now go back and forth. *I want water*?

*[pause]*

**Student:** Malā pāṇī havam.

**Teacher:** *I don't want water*?

*[pause]*

**Student:** Malā pāṇī nako.

**Teacher:** *She wants milk*?

*[pause]*

**Student:** Tilā dūdh havam.

**Teacher:** *She doesn't want milk*?

*[pause]*

**Student:** Tilā dūdh nako.

**Teacher:** Good. The last word flips. Everything else stays.

---

### Track 6 — Close (06:30)

**Teacher:** Let's stop there.

**Teacher:** In about seven minutes you've learned the frame for *I want, you want, he wants, she wants*, and the *don't want* version of all of them. With water, milk, and food. Same frame the whole way. Verb at the end. Pronoun at the front does the work English makes the verb do.

**Teacher:** Here's what I want you to do tonight. Pick one. Just one. *Malā pāṇī havam.* Or *Malā jevaṇ havam.* And say it at the dinner table. Don't announce that you're learning. Just say it. See what happens.

**Teacher:** Next time, we'll meet the polite forms — the ones you use when you're addressing your in-laws or any elder. Until then.

## VOCAB & STRUCTURE INTRODUCED

| Marathi | Roman | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| मला | malā | to me / I (want-subject) |
| तुला | tulā | to you (informal) |
| त्याला | tyālā | to him |
| तिला | tilā | to her |
| पाणी | pāṇī | water (neuter) |
| दूध | dūdh | milk (neuter) |
| जेवण | jevaṇ | food / meal (neuter) |
| हवं | havam | wanted (neuter form) |
| नको | nako | not wanted |

**Structures introduced through pattern, not explanation:**
- SOV word order
- Dative-experiencer construction for *want* (subject takes dative, object stays nominative)
- The *-lā* dative ending visible across pronouns (malā, tulā, tyālā, tilā)
- Negation by particle replacement (havam → nako) rather than insertion
- Gender agreement on *havam* (neuter form here — we deliberately stuck to neuter nouns to avoid teaching gender yet)

**Structures deliberately NOT taught:**
- Polite/formal pronouns (तुम्ही tumhī, आपण āpaṇ) — for Lesson 2, the in-laws lesson
- Masculine / feminine gender agreement on havam (हवा havā / हवी havī) — for Lesson 2 or 3
- The copula आहे (āhe) — we used the colloquial form without it; this is a question for the rater
- Verb-after-verb constructions (I want to eat, I want to sleep) — separate Marathi structure with -ायचं suffix, save for Lesson 2
- Past, future tenses — Lesson 4+

## FOR YOUR SPOUSE / IN-LAW WHEN YOU SHOW THEM THIS

Three questions worth asking, before you trust any of it:

1. **Does *Malā pāṇī havam* sound like real Marathi, or does it sound like a textbook?** Would you actually say this at the dinner table, or would you say *Malā pāṇī havam āhe*, or *Malā pāṇī pāhije*, or something else entirely?
2. **Are the pronoun shifts (tulā, tyālā, tilā) correct in the dative for the *havam* construction?** Or does the form change in ways I'm not capturing?
3. **Is *nako* the natural negative here, or would you use *nāhī*, or something else?**

If they say "this sounds weird but the structure is right," the fix is small. If they say "no one would say it this way," the fix is bigger and we'll need them to dictate the natural version of these sentences for us to rebuild from.
