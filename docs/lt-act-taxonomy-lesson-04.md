## The number that decides the architecture

Count how often each move shows up:

| Move | Times | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Ask them to say it | 24 | 21% |
| Say "Good" | 21 | 18% |
| Repeat their answer back | 18 | 16% |
| Say it properly for their ear | 7 | 6% |
| **those four together** | **70** | **61%** |
| everything else (28 moves) | 44 | 39% |

**Four moves are most of the lesson. Twenty-eight moves are the rest.**

And those four don't need any thinking. If they got it right, you say "Good" and repeat it
back. If it's the next rung on the ladder, you ask the next question. A simple table
handles all of it.

The other 28 are judgment. *Is this the moment for an etymology story? Should I stop and
explain what they just did right?* That's what a model is for.

So the tutor needs both: a lookup table doing the 61%, and a model deciding the 39%.
That wasn't a design choice, it's just what the counting showed.

## Two things I only noticed by doing it by hand

### The three-step ladder repeats exactly

"Cancellation" → "to cancel" → "I want to cancel." Then the identical three steps for
*exploration*, *cooperation*, *preparation*.

That's not three decisions, it's one routine that runs three times. Worth building as a
routine, because a routine needs no thinking at all, and this is the most common stretch
of the whole lesson.

### "Never ask for something untaught" is subtler than it sounds

He asks for *firmar* — a word he has never taught.

It's fair, because he gave them *firma* twenty turns earlier inside a story about where
"confirmation" comes from, and the *-ar* ending came from the rule. They can build it.

So the check isn't **"have I taught this word?"** It's **"can they build this from
pieces I've given them?"** Much harder to get right. Too strict and you block the best
moment in the lesson. Too loose and you're asking for things they can't possibly know.

## Questions for you

Don't worry about the label names. Just read the quotes.

**1. Same move, or two different moves?**

> A: "Good."
> B: "I was tempted to say *confirmation*. But you thought, no…"

If those are the same move, the list is shorter. If B is doing something A isn't, it stays.

**2. Same move, or four different ones?**

> A: "*Confront* comes from forehead."
> B: "*Te amo* is much more serious than *te quiero*."
> C: "This means 'I want to get ready'."
> D: "*Con* becomes M before a P — same in English."

All four are him stepping aside from the drill. One move, or four?

**3. Is the three-step ladder always noun → verb → sentence,** or does it change shape
later in the course?

**4. Does the mix change in harder lessons?** In lesson 4, four moves cover 61%. If that
drops to 40% by lesson 40, the tutor has to think more often, and the whole timing budget
changes.

**5. Am I wrong about any of the 32?** The ones I'm least sure about are
"break a phrase to pull out a piece" and "rewrite the English to match Spanish" — they
might be the same thing wearing two hats.
