Yoruba LT Lessons 1-4 Review Package

Yoruba LT Lessons 1-4 Review Package

Status: draft scripts for native Yoruba review. Not native-certified.

Purpose: review the first four audio-only lessons for grammar, idiom, tone performance, register, and teaching naturalness.

What To Review

Please review Lessons 1-4 in order:

  1. Lesson 1: Mo fẹ́ ..., tone as held pitch, first sentence frame.
  2. Lesson 2: same frame with more endings.
  3. Lesson 3: informal singular o, "you".
  4. Lesson 4: ó, "he/she/it", contrasted with o.

Important Context

This is an audio-only course. The learner will not see Yoruba spelling, tone marks, or written explanations. The marks in the script are for the speaker/reviewer to pronounce the Yoruba correctly.

Where the script says [pause], hold about three seconds. That is where the learner tries to answer before hearing the model.

After every learner answer, the teacher repeats the correct Yoruba answer. This is intentional. The learner needs to hear the correct target again immediately.

What I Need From You

For each lesson, please answer the rater questions at the end of that lesson.

Please especially flag:

  • Any Yoruba that is grammatically wrong.
  • Any sentence that is technically correct but unnatural.
  • Any tone mark that would make you say the wrong word.
  • Any register issue, especially familiar vs respectful forms.
  • Any teacher explanation that would confuse a beginner.
  • Any place where a better everyday Yoruba phrase should replace the draft.

Honest criticism is much more useful than polite approval.

Language Transfer Yoruba — Lesson 1, Draft v5

Status: draft. Not native-certified.

Audience: diaspora heritage learner. English-speaking. Has heard Yoruba at home, never studied it.

Method: Language Transfer / Thinking Method.

Medium: audio only. The learner never sees text.

Target length: 14-17 minutes at slow LT pace.

Main atom: tone as held pitch plus the first productive frame, Mo fẹ́ ....

Teacher stance: the learner is not memorizing Yoruba phrases. They are learning to hold a small frame steady while English tries to interfere.

Likely learner errors:

  • sliding the voice instead of holding pitch,
  • adding English "to" before a second verb,
  • trying to remember the whole sentence instead of rebuilding the frame,
  • flattening fẹ́ after hearing several mid/low words.

Notes for the Yoruba Speaker Rating This

This is an audio-only course. The script is a performance document. The listener hears only your voice. They will not see spelling, tone marks, or Yoruba text.

Please read teacher lines slowly and warmly. Where the script has [pause], hold about three seconds so the learner can try the answer before hearing it.

Critical review questions:

  1. Is Mo fẹ́ jẹun the best beginner sentence for "I want to eat"?
  2. Is oúnjẹ the right beginner word for "food" in this lesson?
  3. Are the pitch descriptions accurate enough for a native speaker to perform?
  4. Does anything sound too formal, too textbook-like, or unnatural for everyday Yoruba?
  5. Does the teacher voice feel like a real patient teacher, not a narrator?

Script

Track 1 — How to Work

Teacher: Welcome. Before we touch Yoruba, I want to set the rules of the game.

Teacher: I will ask you to build something. When I ask, pause the audio. Don't rush. Say your answer out loud. Then come back and listen.

Teacher: If you answer slowly, that is not a problem. That is the method working. Fast guessing feels nice, but slow thinking builds the language.

Teacher: And don't try to memorize. If you catch yourself thinking, "Do I remember this phrase?", gently move away from that. We are going to build the phrase again from smaller pieces.

Teacher: Good. Yoruba now.

Track 2 — The Voice Skill

Teacher: Yoruba is going to ask your voice to do something it already knows how to do.

Teacher: Hum one note, any note, and hold it.

[pause]

Teacher: Good. Now hum a higher note and hold it. Don't slide up to it like a question. Just land there and stay there.

[pause]

Teacher: Good. Now a lower note. Hold that one too.

[pause]

Teacher: That's the useful skill. Not singing. Not performing. Just letting your voice sit somewhere.

Teacher: English uses pitch mostly for attitude: surprise, doubt, a question, maybe sarcasm if you're feeling dramatic. Yoruba can use pitch as part of the word itself.

Teacher: So today, don't try to understand the whole tone system. That would be too much. Just copy where the voice sits.

Track 3 — I

Teacher: The Yoruba word for "I" is mo. Mo.

Teacher: Listen once: mo.

Teacher: Now say it.

Student: Mo.

Teacher: Good. Mo. Small word, middle of the voice. Don't lean on it. It just opens the door for the rest of the sentence.

Teacher: Again: mo.

Student: Mo.

Teacher: Good. Mo.

Track 4 — Want

Teacher: The word for "want" is fẹ́. Fẹ́.

Teacher: Listen before you say it. My voice is sitting higher there: fẹ́.

Teacher: Try it.

Student: Fẹ́.

Teacher: Good. Fẹ́. If it came out a little flat, don't wrestle with it. Just listen again: mo ... fẹ́. Middle, then higher.

Teacher: Mo means I. Fẹ́ means want.

Teacher: Build it in two pieces. How would you say "I want"?

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́.

Teacher: Yes. Mo fẹ́.

Teacher: Good because you did not search for a whole phrase. You put the two pieces next to each other.

Teacher: Say it once more and let the second piece sit higher.

Student: Mo fẹ́.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́.

Track 5 — Water

Teacher: The word for water is omi. Omi.

Teacher: Two little beats, both comfortable in the middle of the voice. Omi.

Teacher: Say it.

Student: Omi.

Teacher: Good. Omi. Now don't remember a phrase. Build it.

Teacher: First the frame: I want.

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́. Now add water. "I want water."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ omi. You kept the frame still and only changed the ending. That's the habit we want.

Teacher: Notice also there was no word for "a." English might say "a water" in some situations, or "some water." Yoruba didn't ask us for that here. Just the thing: omi.

Teacher: Again. "I want water."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ omi.

Track 6 — Food

Teacher: The word for food is oúnjẹ. Oúnjẹ.

Teacher: Listen first. Oúnjẹ.

Teacher: Try it slowly.

Student: Oúnjẹ.

Teacher: Good. Oúnjẹ. If your mouth felt busier there, that's fine. We are not stopping to study every sound. We are just using the word in the frame.

Teacher: How would you say "I want food"?

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ oúnjẹ.

Teacher: Yes. Mo fẹ́ oúnjẹ.

Teacher: What stayed still?

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́.

Teacher: Exactly. Mo fẹ́. The frame stayed still.

Teacher: Now swap only the last piece. "I want water."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: "I want food."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ oúnjẹ.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ oúnjẹ. That is not memorizing. That is control.

Track 7 — Eat

Teacher: Sometimes you want a thing. Water. Food.

Teacher: Sometimes you want to do something. English puts a little "to" there: I want to eat.

Teacher: English is going to try to donate that "to" to Yoruba. Don't accept the gift. Yoruba didn't ask for it here.

Teacher: The word for eat is jẹun. Jẹun.

Teacher: Say it.

Student: Jẹun.

Teacher: Good. Jẹun. So take it in two pieces. First, "I want."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́.

Teacher: Now add eat. "I want to eat."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ jẹun.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ jẹun.

Possible learner mistake: Mo fẹ́ to jẹun.

Teacher: If you felt that English "to" trying to sneak in, excellent, notice it. That's exactly where English interferes. Go back to the Yoruba frame: mo fẹ́, then the action. Mo fẹ́ jẹun.

Teacher: Again. "I want to eat."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ jẹun.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ jẹun.

Track 8 — Sleep

Teacher: One more action. Listen before you say this one.

Teacher: Sùn. Sùn.

Teacher: Let your voice sit low and stay there. Sùn.

Teacher: Try it.

Student: Sùn.

Teacher: Good. Sùn. Now build "I want to sleep."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ sùn.

Teacher: Yes. Mo fẹ́ sùn.

Teacher: Listen to the little path your voice just walked: mo, middle. Fẹ́, higher. Sùn, lower. Three small pieces, and each one has its own place.

Teacher: We are not making a chart of tones. We are letting your ear begin to trust itself.

Track 9 — Mixed Building

Teacher: Now I will mix things and actions. Your job is always the same: find the frame, then add the ending.

Teacher: "I want water."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ omi. The frame held.

Teacher: "I want to eat."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ jẹun.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ jẹun. No English "to."

Teacher: "I want food."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ oúnjẹ.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ oúnjẹ.

Teacher: "I want to sleep."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ sùn.

Teacher: Very good. Mo fẹ́ sùn. Let's do a quicker round, but not rushed. Quick thinking is still thinking.

Teacher: "I want food."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ oúnjẹ.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ oúnjẹ.

Teacher: "I want water."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: "I want to sleep."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ sùn.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ sùn.

Teacher: "I want to eat."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ jẹun.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ jẹun.

Track 10 — Close

Teacher: Good. Let's stop before we get greedy.

Teacher: You have one engine now:

Teacher: Mo fẹ́ ...

Teacher: Mo fẹ́ omi. I want water.

Teacher: Mo fẹ́ oúnjẹ. I want food.

Teacher: Mo fẹ́ jẹun. I want to eat.

Teacher: Mo fẹ́ sùn. I want to sleep.

Teacher: The win is not four phrases. The win is that the frame stayed still while you changed the ending.

Teacher: Next time, we will keep this frame and get more control. No new mountain. Just stronger footing.

Vocabulary and Structure Introduced

Yoruba Meaning Confidence Notes
mo I high subject pronoun
fẹ́ want high needs tone-certified native reading
omi water high source-stable
oúnjẹ food medium verify beginner naturalness
jẹun eat medium verify vs jẹ in this frame
sùn sleep high low tone needs performance check

Structures introduced:

  • tone as held pitch,
  • Mo fẹ́ ...,
  • no indefinite article,
  • same frame for noun endings and verb endings,
  • no English infinitive "to" after fẹ́.

Structures intentionally delayed:

  • o,
  • ó,
  • negation,
  • Ṣé,
  • láti,
  • spelling/diacritics,
  • tense/aspect.

Rater Questions

  1. Scores: grammar __ / idiom __ / tone-when-read-aloud __
  2. Is Mo fẹ́ jẹun the right beginner form for "I want to eat"? If not, what should replace it?
  3. Is oúnjẹ the right beginner word for "food" here?
  4. Does the script explain pitch in a way that helps performance, or would a Yoruba speaker describe it differently?
  5. Does the teacher voice feel alive and useful, or over-written?
  6. Is the amount of repetition right, too much, or too little?
  7. Would you give this to a younger cousin or diaspora learner? Why or why not?

End

Language Transfer Yoruba — Lesson 2, Draft v2

Status: draft. Not native-certified.

Audience: diaspora heritage learner. English-speaking. Has heard Yoruba at home, never studied it.

Method: Language Transfer / Thinking Method.

Medium: audio only. The learner never sees text.

Target length: 13-16 minutes at slow LT pace.

Main atom: same Mo fẹ́ ... frame, more control.

Teacher stance: the learner is learning that repetition can feel like new thought when the frame is reused with intention.

Likely learner errors:

  • trying to recall whole phrases from Lesson 1,
  • adding English "to" before action endings,
  • expecting new grammar because a new lesson has begun,
  • letting tone go flat when attention shifts to meaning.

Notes for the Yoruba Speaker Rating This

This lesson deliberately adds very little grammar. It should feel like control, not expansion.

Please check:

  1. Are lọ, wá, and owó appropriate beginner additions?
  2. Are the tones marked well enough for a native speaker to perform?
  3. Does Mo fẹ́ wá sound natural enough as a beginner drill sentence, or should it be replaced?
  4. Is Mo fẹ́ owó acceptable as a simple drill sentence, or too blunt/odd?
  5. Does the teacher voice feel patient and alive?

Script

Track 1 — Rebuild, Don't Remember

Teacher: Welcome back. We are going to do something very useful today: almost nothing new.

Teacher: That may sound disappointing, but it is actually where a language starts becoming yours.

Teacher: Don't try to remember the phrases from Lesson 1. Rebuild them.

Teacher: What was the little frame for "I want"?

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́. You found the frame.

Teacher: Now add water. "I want water."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ omi. Now sleep. "I want to sleep."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ sùn.

Teacher: Yes. Mo fẹ́ sùn. And notice, no panic if it took a moment. The moment you spent rebuilding is the useful part.

Track 2 — The Frame Holds

Teacher: Say just the frame again.

Student: Mo fẹ́.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́. That is our little machine.

Teacher: Food was oúnjẹ. So "I want food."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ oúnjẹ.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ oúnjẹ.

Teacher: Eat was jẹun. "I want to eat."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ jẹun.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ jẹun. What changed between those two answers?

[pause]

Student: The ending.

Teacher: Exactly. The ending. The ending changed. The frame did not. This is the kind of boring observation that becomes very powerful if you keep making it.

Track 3 — Go

Teacher: A new action now. The word for go is lọ. Lọ.

Teacher: Listen. Lọ. Low voice. Let it sit low.

Teacher: Try it.

Student: Lọ.

Teacher: Good. Lọ. Build "I want to go."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: Yes. Mo fẹ́ lọ.

Possible learner mistake: Mo fẹ́ to lọ.

Teacher: If English gave you a little "to" there, that is the old habit doing its job. We just don't hire it for Yoruba here. Mo fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: Again. "I want to go."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ lọ.

Track 4 — Come

Teacher: The word for come is wá. Wá.

Teacher: This one sits higher. Wá.

Teacher: Try it.

Student: Wá.

Teacher: Good. Wá. Now build "I want to come."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ wá.

Teacher: Yes. Mo fẹ́ wá.

Teacher: Let's keep the contrast alive.

Teacher: "I want to go."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: "I want to come."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ wá.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ wá. Low ending, high ending. Same frame.

Track 5 — Money

Teacher: One new thing. The word for money is owó. Owó.

Teacher: Listen first: owó.

Teacher: Try it.

Student: Owó.

Teacher: Good. Owó. Now, maybe this sentence is a little too honest, but it is structurally useful. "I want money."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ owó.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ owó.

Teacher: We will let a native speaker tell us if this is too blunt as a beginner sentence. For now, it helps us keep practicing a thing after fẹ́.

Track 6 — Things and Actions

Teacher: Let's sort the endings without using grammar words.

Teacher: Omi. Water. A thing.

Teacher: Owó. Money. A thing.

Teacher: Lọ. Go. An action.

Teacher: Wá. Come. An action.

Teacher: Yoruba lets us keep the frame steady for both.

Teacher: "I want water."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: "I want to go."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: "I want money."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ owó.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ owó.

Teacher: "I want to come."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ wá.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ wá. You are not learning a list. You are learning what the frame can tolerate.

Track 7 — A Useful Trap

Teacher: Listen to the English for a moment.

Teacher: I want water.

Teacher: I want money.

Teacher: I want to sleep.

Teacher: I want to go.

Teacher: English changes its little furniture depending on what follows. Yoruba, here, stays calmer.

Teacher: Mo fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: Mo fẹ́ owó.

Teacher: Mo fẹ́ sùn.

Teacher: Mo fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: This is why we are drilling one frame. We want your mind to trust it before we ask it to do anything heavier.

Track 8 — Final Round

Teacher: Final round. Build each one from the frame.

Teacher: "I want to sleep."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ sùn.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ sùn. Low ending.

Teacher: "I want money."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ owó.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ owó.

Teacher: "I want to come."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ wá.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ wá. You kept fẹ́ high even while thinking about the new word.

Teacher: "I want water."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: "I want to go."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: "I want food."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ oúnjẹ.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ oúnjẹ.

Teacher: "I want to eat."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ jẹun.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ jẹun.

Track 9 — Close

Teacher: Good. Today was not about collecting many words. It was about finding the same path again and again without it feeling like memorization.

Teacher: Mo fẹ́ omi. Mo fẹ́ lọ. Mo fẹ́ owó. Mo fẹ́ wá.

Teacher: Next lesson, we change the person at the front. The verb will stay still, which is a small mercy and we will absolutely take it.

Vocabulary and Structure Introduced

Yoruba Meaning Confidence Notes
lọ go medium verify tone/performance
wá come medium verify beginner naturalness in Mo fẹ́ wá
owó money medium verify naturalness in Mo fẹ́ owó

Structures introduced:

  • no new grammar atom beyond stronger control of Mo fẹ́ ...,
  • mixed noun endings and verb endings,
  • continued delay of English infinitive to.

Structures intentionally delayed:

  • o,
  • ó,
  • sí,
  • object pronouns,
  • negation,
  • questions,
  • future máa.

Rater Questions

  1. Scores: grammar __ / idiom __ / tone-when-read-aloud __
  2. Are lọ, wá, and owó good beginner additions here?
  3. Is Mo fẹ́ wá natural enough for the drill? If not, what should replace it?
  4. Is Mo fẹ́ owó too blunt or odd? If so, what noun should replace owó?
  5. Does Lesson 2 feel useful after Lesson 1, or too repetitive?
  6. Does the teacher voice feel alive and useful, or over-written?
  7. Would you give this to a younger cousin or diaspora learner? Why or why not?

Language Transfer Yoruba — Lesson 3, Draft v2

Status: draft. Not native-certified.

Audience: diaspora heritage learner. English-speaking. Has heard Yoruba at home, never studied it.

Method: Language Transfer / Thinking Method.

Medium: audio only. The learner never sees text.

Target length: 13-16 minutes at slow LT pace.

Main atom: o as informal singular "you."

Teacher stance: the learner is learning to change the front of the sentence without disturbing the verb or the ending.

Likely learner errors:

  • changing fẹ́ because English changes "want/wants" later,
  • using informal o too broadly without respecting register,
  • confusing the front pronoun with the object "you,"
  • trying to turn statements into questions too early.

Notes for the Yoruba Speaker Rating This

This lesson introduces informal singular o but does not yet introduce respectful/plural ẹ. Please check whether that is pedagogically acceptable for a heritage learner if the teacher clearly says this is familiar/informal and that a respectful form is coming later.

Critical review questions:

  1. Is o the right form to introduce here?
  2. Are the sample sentences natural?
  3. Does the respect warning feel sufficient without teaching ẹ yet?
  4. Is it okay that the learner can say "you want water" before they can ask "do you want water?"
  5. Does the teacher voice teach the process clearly?

Script

Track 1 — Rebuild the Machine

Teacher: Welcome back. First, prove to yourself that the frame is still there.

Teacher: What was "I want"?

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́. "I want water."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: "I want to go."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ lọ. You rebuilt, you didn't rummage around for a memorized phrase. That is the skill.

Track 2 — The Front Does the Work

Teacher: Today we change the front of the sentence.

Teacher: In English, listen:

Teacher: I want.

Teacher: You want.

Teacher: Nothing happened to "want." Only "I" became "you."

Teacher: Good news: Yoruba is going to let us do something very similar here.

Track 3 — Familiar You

Teacher: The Yoruba word for "you," talking to one person in a familiar way, is o. O.

Teacher: Listen: o.

Teacher: Try it.

Student: O.

Teacher: Good. O. Small word, middle of the voice.

Teacher: Now, careful. This is a familiar "you." Useful, but not for everyone in the family. Yoruba cares about respect, and we will give you that respectful form soon.

Teacher: For today, imagine a sibling, a cousin, a friend. One person, familiar: o.

Track 4 — You Want

Teacher: Build this in two pieces.

Teacher: First, what is "you"?

[pause]

Student: O.

Teacher: Good. O. Now add want. "You want."

[pause]

Student: O fẹ́.

Teacher: Yes. O fẹ́.

Teacher: What stayed still from Mo fẹ́?

[pause]

Student: Fẹ́.

Teacher: Exactly. Fẹ́. That's the win. The person changed and the verb didn't flinch.

Track 5 — I or You

Teacher: Now we swap only the front.

Teacher: "I want water."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: "You want water."

[pause]

Student: O fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: Good. O fẹ́ omi. Same ending. Same verb. Different front.

Teacher: "I want food."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ oúnjẹ.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ oúnjẹ.

Teacher: "You want food."

[pause]

Student: O fẹ́ oúnjẹ.

Teacher: Good. O fẹ́ oúnjẹ.

Possible learner mistake: O fẹ́ to jẹun.

Teacher: If you catch English trying to add "to" later, the fix is the same as before. Front, frame, ending. O fẹ́ jẹun.

Track 6 — Actions

Teacher: Let's use actions now.

Teacher: "I want to sleep."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ sùn.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ sùn.

Teacher: "You want to sleep."

[pause]

Student: O fẹ́ sùn.

Teacher: Good. O fẹ́ sùn. You changed mo to o; everything else survived.

Teacher: "I want to go."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: "You want to go."

[pause]

Student: O fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: Good. O fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: "I want to eat."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ jẹun.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ jẹun.

Teacher: "You want to eat."

[pause]

Student: O fẹ́ jẹun.

Teacher: Very good. O fẹ́ jẹun. That is the frame becoming flexible.

Track 7 — Not a Question Yet

Teacher: A little aside, because English may be getting impatient.

Teacher: "You want water" is a statement. We are not asking "Do you want water?" yet.

Teacher: That question is coming. But if we bring it in now, it will steal attention from today's job.

Teacher: Today's job is simpler and deeper: change the person at the front.

Teacher: Mo fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: O fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: That's enough for one clean thought.

Track 8 — Front and Ending

Teacher: Think of two places.

Teacher: The front tells us who.

Teacher: The ending tells us what is wanted.

Teacher: Build "you want money."

[pause]

Student: O fẹ́ owó.

Language Transfer Yoruba — Lesson 4, Draft v2

Status: draft. Not native-certified.

Audience: diaspora heritage learner. English-speaking. Has heard Yoruba at home, never studied it.

Method: Language Transfer / Thinking Method.

Medium: audio only. The learner never sees text.

Target length: 14-17 minutes at slow LT pace.

Main atom: ó as he/she/it, contrasted with o.

Teacher stance: the learner is learning that tone is not decoration; it can be the difference between two grammatical words.

Likely learner errors:

  • flattening ó into o,
  • over-pronouncing the contrast as a melody instead of a held pitch,
  • expecting separate words for he and she,
  • changing fẹ́ to match English "wants."

Notes for the Yoruba Speaker Rating This

This lesson depends on the performer clearly contrasting o and ó by voice. Please check whether this is safe as an audio-only lesson.

Critical review questions:

  1. Is ó correctly used for he/she/it in these beginner sentences?
  2. Is the o / ó contrast clear and natural enough?
  3. Should ó be introduced as he/she only first, or he/she/it?
  4. Are any examples unnatural without more context?
  5. Does the teacher voice support the tone contrast well?

Script

Track 1 — Rebuild I and You

Teacher: Welcome back. Let's wake up the frame.

Teacher: "I want water."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ omi. "You want water."

[pause]

Student: O fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: Good. O fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: "I want to sleep."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ sùn.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ sùn.

Teacher: "You want to sleep."

[pause]

Student: O fẹ́ sùn.

Teacher: Good. O fẹ́ sùn. Mo at the front for I. O at the front for familiar you.

Teacher: Today we keep that same frame, but your ear has to do slightly finer work.

Track 2 — Listen Before Meaning

Teacher: Listen first. Don't translate yet.

Teacher: O.

Teacher: Ó.

Teacher: Again.

Teacher: O.

Teacher: Ó.

Teacher: The mouth shape is almost the same. The place in the voice is not.

Teacher: O sits in the middle. Ó sits higher.

Teacher: Try them slowly.

Student: O. Ó.

Teacher: Good. O. Ó. If you slid upward like a little question, try again more simply. Land in the middle: o. Land higher: ó.

Track 3 — The New Front

Teacher: You already know o as familiar "you."

Teacher: The higher one, ó, means he, she, or it.

Teacher: Listen again with meaning now.

Teacher: O. You.

Teacher: Ó. He, she, or it.

Teacher: Say them.

Student: O. Ó.

Teacher: Good. O. Ó. This is why we did not want to rush tone. Here it earns its keep.

Track 4 — He or She Wants

Teacher: Build this carefully.

Teacher: If o fẹ́ is "you want," how would you say "he wants"?

[pause]

Student: Ó fẹ́.

Teacher: Yes. Ó fẹ́.

Teacher: What changed?

[pause]

Student: The front.

Teacher: Good. The front. What stayed still?

[pause]

Student: Fẹ́.

Teacher: Exactly. Fẹ́. English says he wants. Yoruba here keeps fẹ́ still. The verb doesn't flinch.

Teacher: And "she wants"?

[pause]

Student: Ó fẹ́.

Teacher: Good. Ó fẹ́. Same Yoruba. Context will tell us whether we mean he or she.

Track 5 — You or He

Teacher: Now we make the contrast work.

Teacher: Listen to the English prompt. Decide whether the front is o or ó.

Teacher: "You want water."

[pause]

Student: O fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: Good. O fẹ́ omi. Middle voice at the front.

Teacher: "He wants water."

[pause]

Student: Ó fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: Good. Ó fẹ́ omi. Higher at the front.

Possible learner mistake: O fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: If you said o fẹ́ omi for "he wants water," the structure was fine. Only the pitch picked the wrong person. That is a good kind of mistake: small, audible, fixable. Ó fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: "You want food."

[pause]

Student: O fẹ́ oúnjẹ.

Teacher: Good. O fẹ́ oúnjẹ.

Teacher: "She wants food."

[pause]

Student: Ó fẹ́ oúnjẹ.

Teacher: Good. Ó fẹ́ oúnjẹ.

Track 6 — Three Fronts

Teacher: Now we have three fronts.

Teacher: Mo. I.

Teacher: O. You.

Teacher: Ó. He or she.

Teacher: Say them slowly.

Student: Mo. O. Ó.

Teacher: Good. Mo. O. Ó. Not a chant, not a song. Just three small words with three jobs.

Teacher: "I want money."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ owó.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ owó.

Teacher: "You want money."

[pause]

Student: O fẹ́ owó.

Teacher: Good. O fẹ́ owó.

Teacher: "She wants money."

[pause]

Student: Ó fẹ́ owó.

Teacher: Good. Ó fẹ́ owó. You changed the person and the rest of the sentence stayed calm.

Track 7 — English Wants to Change the Verb

Teacher: English is going to make one more suggestion now.

Teacher: I want.

Teacher: You want.

Teacher: He wants.

Teacher: English changes want to wants.

Teacher: Yoruba does not need that here.

Teacher: Mo fẹ́.

Teacher: O fẹ́.

Teacher: Ó fẹ́.

Teacher: Same fẹ́ every time.

Teacher: That is a relief. Take the relief where the language gives it to you.

Track 8 — Actions

Teacher: Now with actions.

Teacher: "I want to sleep."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ sùn.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ sùn.

Teacher: "You want to sleep."

[pause]

Student: O fẹ́ sùn.

Teacher: Good. O fẹ́ sùn.

Teacher: "She wants to sleep."

[pause]

Student: Ó fẹ́ sùn.

Teacher: Good. Ó fẹ́ sùn. Hear that high front and low ending: ó ... sùn.

Teacher: "He wants to go."

[pause]

Student: Ó fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: Good. Ó fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: "You want to go."

[pause]

Student: O fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: Good. O fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: "I want to go."

[pause]

Student: Mo fẹ́ lọ.

Teacher: Good. Mo fẹ́ lọ.

Track 9 — It

Teacher: Ó can also mean it.

Teacher: We do not need much of that yet, and I don't want it stealing the lesson. But you should hear the possibility.

Teacher: "It wants water."

[pause]

Student: Ó fẹ́ omi.

Teacher: Good. Ó fẹ́ omi. Same Yoruba sentence as "he wants water" or "she wants water." Context does the choosing.

Teacher: We will leave it there for now. Touched, not unpacked.

Track 10 — Final Round

Teacher: Final round. This is ear work and structure work together.

Teacher: "You want to eat."

[pause]

Teacher: Good. O fẹ́ jẹun.
